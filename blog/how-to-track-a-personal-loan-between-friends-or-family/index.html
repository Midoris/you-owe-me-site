Personal loan recordkeeping

How to Track a Personal Loan Between Friends or Family

Keep one record for the loan and add every real payment as a separate dated entry. Keep the repayment plan beside that record—not in place of it—so you can always see what actually happened, what remains, and what is expected next.

The copyable template below works in Notes or a spreadsheet. If payments have already started, the free browser tracker can calculate the current state.

Use this when repayment is already understood. If it is unclear whether the money was a gift, a loan, or flexible support, clarify that first.

Use this guide for one identifiable loan

This guide is for one personal loan between people who know each other—a laptop purchase, repair, deposit, family loan, or another amount that will be repaid over time. It works whether you lent the money or borrowed it.

The arrangement can be informal, but repayment should already be understood. The purpose of the record is to prevent memory, messages, and payment notifications from turning into different versions of what happened.

The core rule

Record reality. Plan the future.

A clear personal-loan record answers two different questions: What actually happened? What is expected next? Those answers belong together, but they should not be treated as the same thing.

A payment is an event. An installment is an expectation.

Only a real payment reduces the balance. A promised or scheduled installment shows what was expected, but it should never be recorded as paid until the money actually moves.

When one payment is smaller, larger, earlier, or later than planned, record the real event first. Review the future plan separately. That keeps the history honest without pretending that the original schedule still fits perfectly.

Difference between actual loan activity and a repayment plan
Actual recordRepayment plan

History records reality. The plan describes the future. The current status connects them.

Worked example

One loan, several real changes

Alex lent Jordan 1,200 for a laptop. They expected payments of 200 each month, starting on 1 August. The plan describes what should happen. The activity log records what actually happens.

All amounts in this example use one currency.

Actual activity for the 1,200 laptop loan
DateActual eventBalance changeBalance afterWhat the record preserves
Jul 10Laptop loan started+1,2001,200The opening loan remains part of the history.
Aug 1Jordan paid 150−1501,050The payment was 150, even though the expected installment was 200.
Sep 1Jordan paid 300−300750The complete 300 stays visible as one real payment.
Sep 15Jordan borrowed another 100+100850The additional amount is a new event. Earlier payments are not rewritten.

What changes—and what does not

After the 150 payment, do not replace the original 1,200 loan with 1,050. Add the real 150 payment and show the new balance.

The plan may still show that 200 was expected. The missing 50 belongs to the current plan status; it is not a payment that happened.

When Jordan later pays 300, keep the full payment as one real event. A tracker can apply part of it to the earlier shortfall and part to future installments, but the historical payment should still show the amount and date that actually occurred.

When another 100 is borrowed, add it as new activity. The balance becomes 850. The future projection may change, but the earlier loan and payment entries remain visible.

The balance follows real activity. The plan follows the balance.

Record these facts when the loan begins

A useful record does not need to feel formal. It only needs enough context for the amount, history, and next step to remain understandable later.

  1. People and direction

    Record who lent the money and who borrowed it.

  2. Loan name or purpose

    Use a short label such as “Laptop purchase,” “Moving deposit,” or “Bike repair” so this loan is not confused with other money between the same people.

  3. Currency

    Record the currency instead of assuming the symbol will remain obvious later.

  4. Date the money was provided

    Use the real date when the money or purchase was provided, not the date when you finally created the record.

  5. Starting amount

    For a new loan, record the amount provided. For an existing loan, start from the balance that is definitely still owed today.

  6. Repayment expectation

    Record that repayment is understood. If that is still unclear, resolve the gift-versus-loan question before treating the amount as owed.

  7. Current repayment plan, if one exists

    Record the expected amount, frequency, and next date without treating future installments as completed payments.

  8. Where real payments will be recorded

    Choose one reliable record rather than splitting the history across a note, spreadsheet, bank notifications, and several chat threads.

  9. What happens if timing changes

    Agree to record the real payment first and review the future plan separately when circumstances change.

A transfer receipt can show that money moved. A chat can show what someone said. Neither one automatically maintains the remaining balance. Keep them as supporting context, not as the only record.

Add repayments without rewriting the past

Every repayment should become a new dated event. Use the amount that actually moved, add a short note when context may matter later, and update the current balance.

Do not edit the opening loan every time money is repaid. The starting point explains where the loan began; the activity history explains how it reached the current amount.

You do not need to recreate uncertain history before you can begin. Start with the amount that both people can confidently treat as the current remaining balance.

Keep a loan separate when it has its own story

Two people can have more than one kind of money between them. A loan deserves its own record when it has a separate purpose, balance, repayment plan, or settlement point.

A laptop loan and a moving-deposit loan may involve the same two people, but combining them into one unexplained number makes it harder to see which payment belongs where.

This record can

  • Show the current remaining balance
  • Keep the current repayment plan visible

Frequently asked questions

Should I change the original loan amount after each payment?

No. Keep the opening loan or starting balance as part of the history and add each repayment as a new dated event. The current remaining balance changes; the historical starting point does not disappear.

What if a payment is smaller than the planned installment?

Record the amount that was actually paid. Reduce the loan balance only by that real amount, and keep any unpaid part of the expected installment visible in the repayment plan. Do not record the full installment as paid.

What if I do not know the complete earlier payment history?

Begin with the amount that is definitely still owed today. Record the original start date if it is known, and keep any paid-before-tracking amount as historical context only. Do not subtract it from the current balance again or invent uncertain transactions.

Should two separate loans to the same person be combined?

Keep them separate when they have different purposes, repayment plans, interest context, or settlement points. A laptop loan and a moving-deposit loan can each keep their own record while an overall relationship balance shows the combined position.

Do both people need to use the same app?

No. One person can keep the record. When the other person needs visibility, you can share a message, a focused PDF, or an always-current Live Link that opens in a browser without requiring the app or an account.

When is a note enough for a personal loan?

A note may be enough when one clear loan will settle through one predictable payment, both people already understand the arrangement, no reminder is needed, and no future changes are expected. Use a tracker or ongoing app record when payments, corrections, reminders, or plan changes will continue.
